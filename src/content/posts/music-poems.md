During the transition from classical writing to vernacular writing, there was confusion on how to write Chinese poems in the vernacular style. Many poems written in that era sound odd by today's standard.

>假如我是一只鸟，  
>我也应该用嘶哑的喉咙歌唱：  
>这被暴风雨所打击着的土地，  
>这永远汹涌着我们的悲愤的河流，  
>这无止息地吹刮着的激怒的风，  
>和那来自林间的无比温柔的黎明……  
>——然后我死了，  
>连羽毛也腐烂在土地里面。  
>为什么我的眼里常含泪水？  
>因为我对这土地爱得深沉……

That sounds clunky. The words distort the everyday meaning of the words, placed out-of-order, and are long and winding, uncharacteristic of the concise topic-comment structure of the Chinese language. Most critically, it lacks the phrasing that creates musicality. When set to music, as was attempted, it sounds flat to an honest ear.

It has been more than a century, and the debate over how to write poems in the vernacular style has dissipated. Today, people simply write what they feel, relying on AI to compose music and adjust phrasing to fit the rhythm - an instant poem, so to speak.

Yet, there ought to be a framework to guide phrasing. Below are my study notes, written while grappling with a single question: how can we write with prosody?

### The Two Dimensions of Music

A musical composition has two dimensions:

1. Accompaniment (Vertical Dimension): A layer of sounds that acts as the foundation.
    
2. Melody (Horizontal Dimension): The tune that moves over the foundation.
    
Accompaniment consists of:
 
1. Rhythm: The temporal pattern of beats    
      
2. Harmony: The simultaneous combination of pitches in the background

### Musical Instruments vs. Musical Systems

A musical sound is a specific pitch, produced by a key on the instrument (e.g., C, D, E, F, G, A, B).

Musical sounds are mathematically related. A set of related sounds forms a **scale**, and the position of each sound within that scale is its **scale degree** (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 or do-re-mi-fa-so-la-ti).

Once a key note is selected, all musical relationships are governed by the scale set by that key. While a manuscript is played as keys on the instrument, what we actually hear and process is the underlying scale.

### Scales and Modulation

We shift scales by changing the starting key on an instrument, a process known as **modulation**.

1. Dominant Modulation (Up to the 5th degree): Moving from C major to G major (or A minor to E minor) introduces bright, high-tension energy.

2. Subdominant Modulation (Down to the 4th degree): Moving from C major to F major creates a softer, more relaxed expansion.

To vary emotional colour, we can also shift scale modes. Every major key has a **relative minor key** sharing the exact same key signature (e.g., C major shifts to A minor; F major shifts to D minor), allowing transitions between light and dark tonal moods.

### Chord and Harmonic Progression

A **chord** is a set of sounds that sound good together at the same time.  

- Chord Degree (*I*, *IV*, *V*): The scale position where a chord is rooted.
    
- Chord Interval (*1*, *3*, *5*): The relative distances of constituent notes from the chord's root.
    
- Internal Chord Structure: The specific makeup of the chord itself - its base colour (major/minor/diminished/augmented), added tension overtones (sevenths, ninths), and weight distribution (inversions).

Transitions between chords can create tension and resolution. They are called **cadences**. They denote the emotions in the **phrases** and are often considered the puctuation marks of music.

### Tang Poetry as Harmonic Progressions

Tang poetry maps cleanly onto standard harmonic progressions:

# Jueju - A 4-Phrase Prose

- Baseline (_S0_) → Set-off (_S1_) → Peak Tension (_S2_) → Resolution (_S0_)

>春眠不觉晓 Baseline (_S0_)  
>处处闻啼鸟 Set off (_S1_)  
>夜来风雨声 Peak Tension (_S2_)  
>花落知多少 Resolution (_S0_)  

# Lüshi - An Extended Two-Stanza Prose 

Extended progressions allow for longer build-ups, sustained tension, or multi-stanza continuation. An unresolved intermediate state (_S3_) forces the cycle to continue into a second stanza:


>离离原上草 Baseline (_S0_)  
>一岁一枯荣 Set off (_S1_)  
>野火烧不尽 Peak Tension (_S2_)  
>春风吹又生 Suspend (_S3_)  
>远芳侵古道 Baseline (_S0_)  
>晴翠接荒城 Set off (_S1_)  
>又送王孙去 Peak Tension (_S2_)  
>萋萋满别情 Baseline (_S0_)

### Melody and Tones

**The melody** rides above the chords, alternating between alignment (consonance) and tension (dissonance).

- Chord-Tone Alignment: When the foundation rests in a stable state (_S0_ or _S1_), the melodic notes belong directly to the active chord (such as 1, 3, or 5).
    
- Chord-Tone Tension: During peak tension or suspension (_S2_ or _S3_), the melody relies on **non-code tones** (e.g. 4 or 6)  or **unstable chord tones** (e.g. 2 or 7). 

Melodic movement can be modulated using specific techniques:

- Passing Tones: Moving smoothly between chord tones (e.g., stepping through 2 to get from 1 to 3). Its transient duration prevents instability.
- Suspension (Delay): Holding a note from the _previous_ chord state into the _current_ chord, creating a delay that craves resolution.
- Anticipation (Advance): Sounding a note from the _upcoming_ chord early, creating urgency and momentum.
- Appoggiatura (Bang): Striking an accented non-chord tone directly on a strong beat to create sudden tension before resolving into a chord tone.

### Rhythm

**Beats** can be strong or weak. They are grouped into bars with consistent rhythmic patterns (e.g., `Strong - Weak - Strong - Weak`).

Harmonic shifts (_S0_ → _S1_ → _S2_) must align with strong beats.

Passing notes, suspensions, and anticipations land on weak beats to build forward momentum.

Appoggiaturas (bangs) land on strong beats to deliver a deliberate acoustic shock. Light function words (的, 了, 着) naturally belong to weak beats, driving momentum toward the accented words on the strong beats.

By default, musical phrasing is organized into even-numbered bars (4, 8, or 16 bars). A standard 4-bar phrase splits neatly into a **2-bar call** and a **2-bar response**:

- **Bar 1-2**: State initiation (_S0_ → _S1_)
    
- **Bar 3-4**: Peak tension and reset (_S2_ → _S0_)

Poetry recitation must honour this rhythm; reading words verbatim results in a blocky, unnatural delivery. For example, the following naturally fits a 4/4 time (4 beats per bar, two subdivisions per beat):

>春眠 | 不觉 | 晓   | ——  Bar 1: initiate idea; neutral tension (_S0_)  
>处处 | 闻啼 | 鸟   | ——  Bar 2: expand idea; forward momentum (_S1_)  
>夜来 | 风雨 | 声   | ——  Bar 3: peak emotional tension (_S2_)  
>花落𝄐 | 知多 | 少   | ——  Bar 4: response / cadence (_S0_)

Here, a **pause mark 𝄐** has been added to the first word in the last phrase to create a brief moment of hesitation to build a dramatic high tension before its full release in the final line.

### A Structural Rewrite of I Love This Land by Ai Qing

Here is a structural rewrite of Ai Qing’s _I Love This Land_ around two or three characters per beat, explicit cadence, and a clear harmonic progression: _S0_ → _S1_ → _S2_ → _S3_ → _S2_ → _S0_.

#### Stanza 1: Initiation & Expansion (_S0_ → _S1_)

> 假若 | 我是 | 一只鸟， | ——  
> 我必 | 嘶哑 | 歌唱： | ——  
> 唱这 | 风雨 | 打击的 | 土地， | ——  (5 beats instead of 4, or 长短句)  
> 唱这 | 汹涌 | 悲愤的 | 河流…… | ——

#### Stanza 2: Peak Tension & Suspension (_S2_ → _S3_)

> 吹刮着 | 激怒的 | 狂风， | ——  
> 破晓出 | 温柔的 | 黎明。 | ——  
> ——而后 | —— | 我死， | ——  
> 羽毛 | 腐烂 | 泥土中。 | ——  

#### Stanza 3: Call and Response (_S2_ → _S0_)

> 问我 | 眼中 | 何以 | 含泪？ | ——  
> 因我𝄐 | 爱这𝄐 | 土地𝄐 | 深𝄐——  | 沉𝄐。——
